# Mutating Asp120 costs 10 to 10,000-fold in kcat across MBL subclasses, including B3

Read 2026-11-06, not re-derived.

- **L1 (B3, the closest subclass to CSR-1)** - Garrity et al., *J Biol Chem*
  2004: Asp120 mutants show **10- to 1000-fold drops in kcat** against wild type.
  The authors assign the residue a role in forming the bridging hydroxide and in
  a rate-limiting proton transfer.
- **IMP-1 (B1)** - Yamaguchi et al., *J Biol Chem* 2005: mutants lose
  **10^2-10^4-fold in kcat while both zincs remain bound**, and stopped-flow pH
  work argues the residue is neither the thing lowering the bridging water's pKa
  nor a proton donor to the anionic intermediate.
- **BcII (B1)** - Llarrull et al., *J Biol Chem* 2007: proposes Asp120 acts
  mainly as a strong Zn2 ligand, positioning that ion for substrate binding and
  for protonation of the beta-lactam nitrogen by a zinc-bound water.

**How this bears on the thesis, both ways.** It establishes that this
carboxylate's *position* is worth a large factor in rate, which is the premise
behind caring what holds it - and the mainstream explanation is that it holds
**Zn2**, which is precisely the ion CSR-1 does not have. So in CSR-1 something
else must hold the carboxylate, and the lysine is the candidate
([[lys226-salt-bridges-asp92-in-wild-type-crystals]]).

**The part that cuts against us.** IMP-1 keeps both zincs and still loses
10^2-10^4-fold. Whatever the aspartate does beyond positioning a metal, it is
large, and nothing in our argument says CSR-1's lysine can supply it. That is
named in the thesis as the third way it could be wrong.

**Primary sources not yet read in full** - these are abstracts and secondary
summaries. Before any of it goes in a paper the three papers need reading, and
the L1 numbers in particular, since L1 is the B3 comparator a referee will reach
for.
