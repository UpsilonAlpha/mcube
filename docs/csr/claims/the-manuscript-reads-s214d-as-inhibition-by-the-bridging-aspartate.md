# The manuscript's own reading of S214D is inhibition by the aspartate, and it calls the position metal-bridging

Three places, verbatim.

**The term.** The anti-Pycsar nucleases "natively possess a **metal-bridging
aspartate residue in this position**" - so *bridging* is the manuscript's own
word for this position, which also settles the ¶268 terminology question.

**The alternative, stated as a fork:**

> these results suggest either that the serine is critical to the catalysis of
> penicillins, **or that the aspartate residue directly prevents binding or
> inhibits hydrolysis** of these antibiotics

The paper then undercuts the first branch with Hou et al. 2017's S-to-A
retaining activity ([[s214d-cannot-isolate-the-serine-role]]), which leaves the
aspartate-inhibits branch standing. For cephalosporins it proposes that "the
aspartate mutation has similarly resulted in cefuroxime binding in an
**inhibitory conformation**".

**The structural half matches.** The abstract says the mutant structures
"highlight changes in the **metal ion coordination environment** that may play
an important role in the switch in functionality", and our one-metal S214D
original shows what that means: the single zinc at M2 with **three** waters,
six-coordinate ([[whenever-a-canonical-m2-exists-the-metal-takes-m2]]).

**So S214D is a coherent experiment - a different one.** It asks what an
aspartate at the bridging position does to metal coordination and turnover, not
what a monometallic M1 site does mechanistically. Using it as the mutant arm of
a mechanism comparison would answer the first question while being read as an
answer to the second.
